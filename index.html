My Favorite Literary Masterpiece

The work that left the deepest mark on my literary journey

My Favorite ✦

To Kill a Mockingbird

by Harper Lee · Published 1960 · Pulitzer Prize for Fiction, 1961

📖 Brief Overview

Set in Maycomb, Alabama during the 1930s, the novel is narrated by young Scout Finch, whose father Atticus — a lawyer — defends Tom Robinson, a Black man falsely accused of assault. The story weaves together childhood innocence, racial injustice, and moral courage in the deeply divided American South. Alongside the trial runs the mysterious story of Boo Radley, a reclusive neighbor who becomes an unexpected hero.

💛 Why I Chose This Work

Out of all seven books, this one hit the hardest. I think it's because it doesn't try to sugarcoat anything — it shows injustice as it really is, through the eyes of a child who doesn't yet understand why adults let unfair things happen. As an IT student, I know systems can be biased too, and this novel reminded me that fairness isn't automatic — someone has to fight for it.

🌿 Themes & Characters

Racial Injustice Moral Courage Loss of Innocence Empathy Justice & Law Childhood Perspective

Atticus Finch is one of literature's most memorable characters — not because he's perfect, but because he stands for what's right even when no one else will. His simple advice to Scout — "You never really understand a person until you climb into their skin and walk around in it" — is the kind of line that stays with you long after you close the book.

✨ Personal Connection

This novel made me think about what kind of person I want to be in my field. Technology can either reinforce inequality or help fight it — and Harper Lee reminded me that silence in the face of injustice is a choice too. Atticus chose to stand up even when he knew he'd lose, and that kind of courage is something I really admire and want to carry with me.

Summary of Learning

What the Great Books course taught me

✦ Summary ✦

Taking the Great Books course turned out to be one of the most eye-opening academic experiences I have had so far. I honestly did not expect to connect with texts from ancient India, Greece, medieval France, and Heian Japan, but every single work we read had something relevant to say about the world we live in today. Reading The Panchatantra taught me that wisdom does not always come from grand gestures — sometimes it is hidden in the smallest stories told to the simplest audience. The Odyssey showed me that the desire to come home and find your place in the world is something every person in every era understands. The Song of Roland was eye-opening in a different way because it made me aware of how literature can be used to justify violence and prejudice, and as a Filipino, that felt personal to me. The Tale of Genji surprised me the most — I did not expect a novel written in 11th-century Japan by a woman to feel so emotionally honest and modern. Pride and Prejudice was funny but also sharp in its observation of how money and class shape the choices people think they are freely making. Les Misérables reminded me that systems — legal, economic, social — can be deeply unfair, and that one act of mercy can break a cycle of cruelty. And To Kill a Mockingbird, my favorite, brought everything together: justice, empathy, moral courage, and the painful reality of what happens when society chooses comfort over truth. What I take away most from this course is that great literature is not just about the past. It is a mirror that reflects who we are right now — and a challenge to be better.

Analysis No. 01

The Panchatantra

Attributed to Vishnu Sharma · Ancient India
Moral / Philosophical Lens

Introduction

The Panchatantra is one of the oldest surviving collections of instructive fables in world literature, believed to have been composed in Sanskrit around 200 BCE–300 CE and attributed to the scholar Vishnu Sharma. Translated into more than fifty languages and adapted across cultures from Persia to medieval Europe, the work presents its wisdom through a frame narrative: a king hires Vishnu Sharma to educate three dim-witted princes in the science of practical statecraft and virtuous living. The vehicle he chooses is animal fables — stories in which lions, crows, mice, and monkeys act out the moral lessons of human society with remarkable clarity.

Moral / Philosophical Lens

Analyzing the Panchatantra through the Moral/Philosophical lens reveals a carefully constructed ethical system rooted in the ancient Indian concept of nīti — the science of wise conduct and prudent governance. Unlike Western moral philosophy, which often seeks universal abstract principles, the Panchatantra's ethics are fundamentally pragmatic and situational. Morality is not simply about what is universally right, but about what wisdom requires in a given context. The collection is organized into five books, each addressing a different dimension of practical wisdom.

Analysis

The most philosophically significant dimension of the Panchatantra is its insistence that wisdom is inseparable from discernment — the ability to read situations accurately and respond with appropriate judgment rather than rigid rule-following. In the story of the lion and the hare, for instance, the small hare defeats a powerful lion not through strength but through wit, using the lion's own vanity against him. The moral lesson is not simply "cleverness beats strength" but something more nuanced: in a world governed by power imbalances, the powerless must cultivate cunning as a survival strategy.

Furthermore, the Panchatantra presents friendship as one of the highest moral goods. The first book's exploration of the friendship between a lion and a bull — and its corruption by a treacherous jackal — demonstrates how envy and self-interest can destroy what is most valuable. The philosophical implication is that virtue is fundamentally social: it requires the cultivation of trustworthy relationships, honest counsel, and the discipline to resist those who would sow discord for personal gain.

Conclusion

The Panchatantra remains a remarkable philosophical achievement because it democratizes wisdom. Its animal characters allow moral instruction to transcend caste, class, and literacy — anyone who hears these stories can absorb their lessons. Through the Moral/Philosophical lens, we see that this ancient text is not simply a collection of clever stories but a comprehensive guide to living well in a complex, often unjust world.

Analysis No. 02

The Odyssey

Homer · Ancient Greece, circa 8th century BCE
Historical / Biographical Lens

Introduction

The Odyssey is one of the foundational texts of Western literature, an epic poem attributed to Homer and composed in ancient Greece around the 8th century BCE. It follows the hero Odysseus on his ten-year journey home to Ithaca after the fall of Troy, a voyage plagued by divine wrath, mythological monsters, seductive enchantresses, and the constant pull of nostalgia and longing. The poem is simultaneously an adventure story, a meditation on heroism, and a profound exploration of identity, home, and the relationship between mortals and their gods.

Historical / Biographical Lens

The Historical/Biographical lens examines literature in relation to the author's life and historical context, as well as the broader cultural and social forces that shaped the work's production and reception. Applied to The Odyssey, this approach requires us to situate the epic within the world of archaic Greece — a civilization organized around city-states, aristocratic warrior culture, the institution of xenia (guest-friendship), and a polytheistic religious system in which the gods intervened directly in human affairs.

Analysis

The society depicted in The Odyssey is fundamentally an oral one. The poem itself was likely composed and transmitted orally before being written down, which explains its characteristic features: repetitive epithets ("rosy-fingered Dawn," "much-enduring Odysseus"), formulaic phrases, and an episodic structure designed to aid memory and performance. Through the historical lens, these are not stylistic weaknesses but functional necessities — the technologies of oral culture pressed into the service of communal memory.

Historicizing the epic also illuminates its vision of heroism. Odysseus is not the straightforward warrior-hero of The Iliad's Achilles; he is cunning, adaptive, and sometimes deceptive — qualities that reflect the values of a seafaring, mercantile culture dependent on intelligence and adaptability as much as military prowess. Penelope's famous ruse — weaving and unraveling a shroud to delay her suitors — has often been read as mere cleverness, but in historical context, it reflects the severely constrained agency available to elite women in ancient Greek society.

Conclusion

Reading The Odyssey through the Historical/Biographical lens transforms it from a timeless adventure into a richly specific cultural document. We see in it the values, anxieties, and social structures of archaic Greek civilization — a world where hospitality was sacred law, the gods were dangerously capricious, and the greatest heroism lay not in dying gloriously in battle but in surviving the long, difficult journey home.

Analysis No. 03

The Song of Roland

Anonymous · Medieval France, circa 11th century
Post-Colonial Lens

Introduction

The Song of Roland is the oldest surviving major work of French literature, composed around the 11th century and rooted in an event from 778 CE. In the poem, the historical defeat at Roncevaux is transformed: the attackers become Saracens (Muslim warriors), and the story becomes an epic of Christian martyrdom, feudal loyalty, and holy war. The hero Roland, proud and unyielding, refuses to call for reinforcements until it is too late, dying with his sword and his faith intact.

Post-Colonial Lens

Post-colonial theory examines how literature participates in the construction and legitimation of colonial power — specifically how texts create, reinforce, and naturalize hierarchies between the colonizer and the colonized. Central to this analysis is Said's concept of "Orientalism": the systematic misrepresentation of Eastern cultures as monolithic, irrational, and inferior, in contrast to a rational, progressive, and culturally superior West.

Analysis

The Song of Roland is a foundational Orientalist text. Its representation of the Saracens is not merely inaccurate — it is ideologically constructed to serve the political and religious needs of medieval Christian Europe at the time of the Crusades. The poem's Saracens are uniformly evil: where Roland is loyal, brave, and self-sacrificing, the Saracen King Marsile is treacherous, cowardly, and faithless; where Christianity is associated with light, rationality, and divine favor, Islam is associated with darkness, deceit, and demonic influence.

As a Filipino reader, the post-colonial lens generates uncomfortable resonances. The Philippines has been subject to its own Orientalizing discourses: American colonial texts depicted Filipinos as childlike, irrational, and in need of "civilizing" — using almost identical rhetorical strategies to those employed in The Song of Roland against the Saracens. Reading Roland through this lens is not merely an academic exercise; it is a confrontation with how literary texts participate in the legitimation of violence.

Conclusion

A post-colonial reading of The Song of Roland reveals the poem as an ideological document — a carefully crafted construction of Christian supremacy and Muslim inferiority designed to mobilize a society for holy war. Recognizing this demands that we read it with critical awareness of the real-world consequences that such representations historically produced.

Analysis No. 04

The Tale of Genji

Murasaki Shikibu · Heian Japan, circa 11th century
Feminist / Gender Lens

Introduction

The Tale of Genji, written by the lady-in-waiting Murasaki Shikibu around 1008 CE, is widely considered the world's first novel. Set in the imperial court of Heian Japan, it follows the romantic and political adventures of Hikaru Genji, the emperor's son, and later the fortunes of his descendants. The novel is celebrated for its extraordinary psychological depth, its aesthetic sensitivity (mono no aware), and its unprecedented exploration of inner life — crucially, written by a woman in an era that restricted female scholarship.

Feminist / Gender Lens

Feminist literary criticism examines how texts construct, challenge, or reinforce gender roles and power relationships. It asks: Whose perspective dominates the narrative? How are women represented, and by whom? A feminist reading of The Tale of Genji is especially productive because the text occupies a paradoxical position: authored by a woman, centered largely on women's experiences, yet narrating a world in which women are entirely subordinate to male authority.

Analysis

Heian court culture was organized around radical gender stratification. Women of the aristocratic class were expected to remain hidden behind screens and curtains, rarely seen in public, their social value determined primarily by their physical beauty, poetic skill, and sexual availability to men of appropriate rank. Yet within these constraints, Murasaki Shikibu creates female characters of remarkable interior depth and complexity.

Lady Murasaki — the young girl Genji "cultivates" into his ideal companion — has been read by feminist scholars as a figure of profound ambiguity: simultaneously victim of Genji's possessiveness and a woman who develops genuine intellectual and artistic mastery. Her eventual emotional withdrawal and decline can be read as a feminist critique of Genji's treatment of her — a quiet, devastating protest against a system that reduces women to objects of male cultivation and desire.

Conclusion

A feminist reading of The Tale of Genji reveals a text of extraordinary complexity: one that simultaneously reflects the patriarchal structures of Heian court life and subtly indicts them through the depth of its female characterization, the interiority of its narration, and the fact of its female authorship. It is a text that invites us to listen for the voices that the dominant culture tried to silence.

Analysis No. 05

Pride and Prejudice

Jane Austen · England, 1813
Marxist / Sociological Lens

Introduction

Pride and Prejudice, published in 1813, is Jane Austen's most celebrated novel. The story follows the five Bennet sisters — particularly the witty and perceptive Elizabeth Bennet — as they navigate the social and marital landscape of Regency-era England. The novel's central romantic plot, the antagonistic courtship between Elizabeth and the proud Mr. Darcy, unfolds against a backdrop of economic anxiety, social hierarchy, and the constrained choices available to women in a world where marriage is the primary means of financial security.

Marxist / Sociological Lens

Marxist literary criticism examines literature in relation to economic structures, class conflict, and the ideological systems through which dominant classes maintain their power. Applied to Pride and Prejudice, this lens transforms what might appear to be a romantic comedy into a sharp sociological document of the economic anxieties of the English middle class.

Analysis

The economic stakes of Pride and Prejudice are made explicit from the novel's famous opening sentence: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." Read through a Marxist lens, this ironic declaration is not merely a clever joke but a precise statement of market conditions: wealthy men are commodities, and women are the competing agents seeking to acquire them.

The character of Charlotte Lucas embodies this reality most clearly. When Charlotte accepts the pompous Mr. Collins — a man Elizabeth has already rejected — she does so with clear-eyed pragmatism: at twenty-seven and without Elizabeth's advantages, she understands that marriage is her only viable alternative to economic dependence. The relationship between Darcy and Elizabeth can itself be read as a class negotiation: Elizabeth gains economic security and social elevation; Darcy gains the intelligence and genuine feeling that his class's self-satisfaction has stunted.

Conclusion

A Marxist reading of Pride and Prejudice reveals Austen's novel as a work of incisive social realism dressed in the costume of romantic comedy. Far from escaping into fantasy, Austen maps with forensic precision the economic structures that determine her characters' lives — and the complex negotiations by which individuals attempt to secure dignity and happiness within constraints they did not choose.

Analysis No. 06

Les Misérables

Victor Hugo · France, 1862
Structuralist Lens

Introduction

Les Misérables, published by Victor Hugo in 1862, is one of the longest, most ambitious, and most morally serious novels in the Western tradition. Spanning nearly two decades of French history, the novel follows the former convict Jean Valjean as he transforms himself through mercy and moral struggle, pursued relentlessly by Inspector Javert. Through Valjean's story and the interwoven fates of Fantine, Cosette, Marius, and others, Hugo constructs a comprehensive indictment of poverty, criminal justice, and social inequality in 19th-century France.

Structuralist Lens

Structuralism examines literature as a system of signs organized by deep structural patterns — particularly binary oppositions. A structuralist reading identifies the fundamental contrasts that organize a text's meaning: good/evil, law/crime, freedom/imprisonment, mercy/justice. It asks: What underlying structure generates the surface narrative? How do these oppositions interact and potentially deconstruct each other?

Analysis

The organizing binary opposition of Les Misérables is the confrontation between law and mercy, embodied in Inspector Javert and Jean Valjean. Javert represents the law in its purest, most unyielding form — law as an absolute system of classification that divides humanity into criminals and citizens, with no provision for transformation or redemption. Valjean represents the opposing principle: mercy, which acknowledges human complexity and the possibility of change.

The structuralist insight is that these oppositions deconstruct each other. When Valjean spares Javert's life at the barricade, Javert's binary system breaks down: a criminal has demonstrated more moral nobility than the law's representative. Unable to integrate this experience into his rigid framework, Javert commits suicide — the ultimate structuralist collapse of the system that organized his identity. Hugo's entire novel argues that the binary oppositions through which society organized its treatment of the poor are structurally inadequate to the complexity of human experience.

Conclusion

A structuralist reading of Les Misérables reveals Hugo's novel as a systematic deconstruction of the binary oppositions through which 19th-century French society organized its treatment of the dispossessed. By showing how every apparent opposition contains its own internal contradiction, Hugo argues that true justice requires a more complex, more compassionate framework than the law alone can provide.

Analysis No. 07

To Kill a Mockingbird

Harper Lee · United States, 1960
Psychoanalytic / Freudian Lens

Introduction

To Kill a Mockingbird, published by Harper Lee in 1960 and awarded the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1961, is set in the fictional town of Maycomb, Alabama during the 1930s. The novel is narrated retrospectively by Jean Louise "Scout" Finch, a young girl growing up in the racially divided American South. The central event of the novel is the trial of Tom Robinson, a Black man falsely accused of assault. Scout's father, Atticus Finch, serves as his defense attorney, knowing the outcome is already decided by a prejudiced community.

Psychoanalytic / Freudian Lens

Psychoanalytic literary criticism examines literature in terms of unconscious processes, repression, and the dynamics of the psyche. It asks: What does the narrative reveal about the characters' unconscious desires and fears? A psychoanalytic reading of To Kill a Mockingbird focuses particularly on the childhood narrator's consciousness as the medium through which adult social pathologies are refracted and gradually understood.

Analysis

Scout Finch's child narrator functions, in psychoanalytic terms, as a relatively undefended consciousness — one that perceives social reality with the uncomfortable accuracy of someone who hasn't yet internalized the community's repressive norms. Her questions repeatedly expose the gap between Maycomb's self-image as a decent community and its operational reality as a society organized around the violent suppression of its Black citizens.

The character of Boo Radley functions as a figure of the repressed — the community's anxieties about deviance projected onto a reclusive neighbor. When Boo emerges to save the children, his rescue reveals the projection as a defense mechanism — the "monster" turns out to be the community's most quietly compassionate member. Tom Robinson's conviction can be read through the concept of the scapegoat: the community cannot acknowledge his innocence without acknowledging its own complicity in a system of racial oppression, and it refuses that acknowledgment.

Conclusion

A psychoanalytic reading of To Kill a Mockingbird illuminates how societies maintain ideological systems they cannot consciously acknowledge without self-destruction. Scout's child consciousness serves as the analyst's space on which Maycomb's unconscious is exposed, and the trial's unjust verdict represents the community's collective choice to destroy an innocent man rather than confront its own moral failure.
